$5.25 to sell. $2.25 to buy. Same mug — owned for just five minutes.

Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler's famous mug study. One group got a mug and were told they owned it. What's the minimum you'd sell for? Median answer: $5.25. Another group saw the same mug. What's the maximum you'd pay? Median answer: $2.25. Same object. 2x valuation gap. Created by five minutes of ownership.

That's the endowment effect. The moment something becomes yours, your brain inflates its value — driven by the same loss aversion that makes losing hurt twice as much as gaining feels good. Selling your mug means losing it. That loss is processed by the same system that makes losing $50 hurt twice as much as gaining $50 feels good. This is why you have things in your home you'd never pay to buy back at the price you'd need to sell them for. Why houses sit on the market too long. Why free trials work — because once you've used it, it's yours, and cancelling feels like a loss. The product didn't change. Your brain's valuation of it did.

Ask yourself: if I didn't own this and saw it for sale today, would I buy it at the price I'd need to sell it for? If no — that's the endowment talking.
